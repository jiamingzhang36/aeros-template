Executive Summary Working title: Dynamic Information Customization and Food Choice: An Audit of AI-Generated Grocery Recommendations. This project studies a new form of food information: AI-generated grocery advice. Unlike static nutrition labels, AI recommendations are personalized, adaptive, and sometimes non-reproducible. The same consumer profile may receive different shopping baskets across repeated interactions, models, or prompts. This creates a new food policy question: before AI tools are used to support healthier food choice, SNAP shopping assistance, or online grocery guidance, do they generate recommendations that are stable, affordable, constraint-compliant, and equitable? The proposed paper does not require a human-subject experiment in the first stage. The main data are AI-generated grocery baskets. Each observation is a combination of persona, model, prompt, and repeated run. The paper audits the outputs directly. This design is faster than a discrete choice experiment, avoids the immediate need for IRB, and turns the dynamic nature of AI into the object of analysis rather than treating it as a nuisance. The most important conceptual move is to extend the food information literature from disclosure to algorithmic intermediation. Nutrition labels disclose product attributes. AI assistants interpret information, rank alternatives, and construct shopping baskets. This shift matters for food policy because AI-generated advice may reduce search costs and cognitive burden, but it may also produce unstable, expensive, unrealistic, or unequal recommendations for low-income and SNAP-relevant households. One-sentence paper idea We audit whether AI-generated grocery recommendations are stable, affordable, nutritionally reasonable, and equitable across realistic household constraints, with special attention to low-income and SNAP-relevant food choice environments. 2. Motivation and Link to Existing Literature Balcombe, Fraser, Lowe, and Souza Monteiro (2016) study information customization and food choice using a hypothetical discrete choice experiment. Their central insight is that consumers may value individual-specific food information more than generic nutrition labels. That article was written before contemporary generative AI became a consumer-facing food choice tool. In the AI era, customization is no longer limited to alerts such as allergy warnings or diet-specific labels. AI systems can generate entire grocery baskets, explain substitutions, rank products, and personalize advice by budget, health condition, cooking constraints, language, and shopping access. This project keeps the same broad theme, information customization and food choice, but changes the object of study. The object is no longer willingness to pay for a hypothetical information service. The object is the quality, stability, and equity of the customized information itself. This is an important distinction. If AI advice is unstable, violates user constraints, or gives systematically worse recommendations to constrained households, then AI-based food information cannot be evaluated like a conventional label. The project is also connected to the food access and low-income food choice literature. Low-income households face budget, transportation, store access, time, and cooking constraints. A recommendation that is nutritionally sound but too expensive, not SNAP-eligible, unavailable at common retailers, or difficult to prepare may be policy-irrelevant. Therefore, this project evaluates not only healthfulness but also feasibility and affordability. Finally, the project connects to emerging work on LLM reliability and bias. Recent research has started to examine the accuracy, consistency, and demographic bias of AI-generated nutrition or meal recommendations. However, most existing work does not place grocery advice inside realistic food policy constraints such as SNAP eligibility, weekly food budgets, limited cooking capacity, or low-access shopping environments. That is the opening for this paper. 3. Research Gap and Contribution The paper should not claim to be the first study of AI nutrition advice. That would be too broad and likely incorrect. The contribution should be narrower and stronger. Proposed contribution: We study dynamic information customization as a new food policy problem. AI-generated food advice is not a fixed disclosure but an adaptive output that varies across consumers, prompts, models, and repeated interactions. We audit AI-generated grocery baskets under realistic constraints, including budget, SNAP-like eligibility, health conditions, cooking capacity, and food access. We measure output quality using economic and policy-relevant outcomes: stability, budget compliance, healthfulness, affordability, feasibility, transparency, and equity. We provide an empirical framework for evaluating AI food assistants before they are integrated into grocery platforms, public health interventions, or nutrition assistance programs. The paper can be framed as a measurement and policy audit paper rather than a consumer demand paper. This is important for feasibility. A human-subject experiment can be a future extension, but the first paper can already make a substantive contribution by showing whether AI-generated grocery advice is reliable and policy-relevant. Research Questions and Hypotheses The paper can be organized around five research questions. Research question Operational interpretation Expected hypothesis RQ1: Stability Does the same persona receive similar grocery baskets across repeated AI interactions? AI outputs will show non-trivial within-persona variation, especially for open-ended prompts. RQ2: Constraint compliance Does AI respect budget, SNAP-like eligibility, health, and cooking constraints? Constraint violations will be more common when prompts are broad and when personas have multiple constraints. RQ3: Healthfulness Are recommended baskets nutritionally reasonable? AI will often recommend broadly healthy items but may fail on sodium, added sugar, or realistic serving composition. RQ4: Affordability and feasibility Are recommended baskets affordable and practical for low-income households? Healthy recommendations may become expensive or unrealistic unless budget and access constraints are explicitly specified. RQ5: Equity Do constrained personas receive systematically worse recommendations? Low-income, SNAP, no-car, rural, and limited-cooking personas may receive less feasible or less stable recommendations than middle-income baseline personas. 5. Conceptual Framework The conceptual framework starts from a simple food choice problem. A household chooses a grocery basket subject to income, time, health, access, and information constraints. Traditional food labels reduce information asymmetry by disclosing product attributes. AI systems do something stronger: they convert product attributes and household constraints into ranked recommendations or complete baskets. Therefore, AI changes the choice environment in at least three ways. Information cost: AI may reduce the burden of reading nutrition panels, comparing ingredients, and interpreting health claims. Search cost: AI may reduce the burden of searching across products, prices, and stores. Choice architecture: AI may steer consumers by ranking some foods first, presenting certain substitutions as better, or framing tradeoffs in persuasive language. The key conceptual claim is that AI recommendations are dynamic information treatments. A static label is identical across consumers. An AI recommendation can vary by persona, prompt wording, model, temperature, available product database, and repeated interaction. This violates the stable-treatment intuition behind many traditional food information studies. For policy analysis, the goal is not simply to ask whether AI can generate healthy advice. The question is whether AI-generated advice is reliable enough to function as a food policy tool. If recommendations are unstable, frequently exceed budgets, ignore SNAP-like constraints, or systematically disadvantage constrained households, then AI-based food guidance may require evaluation standards before deployment. Core conceptual sentence AI changes food information policy from static disclosure to dynamic algorithmic intermediation. 6. Research Design The proposed design is an algorithm audit. The unit of observation is an AI-generated grocery basket. Each basket is produced under a controlled combination of persona, model, prompt, and repeated run. Observation unit: persona × model × prompt type × run The main design should use a fixed product database. The database can begin with 100 to 200 common grocery products, covering staple categories such as grains, dairy, protein, fruits, vegetables, beverages, snacks, frozen meals, canned foods, and prepared foods. Each product should have a category, price, package size, basic nutrition, SNAP-like eligibility classification, and preparation burden. A fixed database prevents AI from inventing products and makes the results auditable. A practical design is: 12 personas × 3 models × 3 prompt types × 20 repeated runs = 2,160 AI-generated baskets. A smaller pilot can use 6 personas × 2 models × 2 prompt types × 10 repeated runs = 240 baskets. A clean version could be: Sample structured prompt Task: Build a one-week grocery basket using only the products listed below. Household: one adult and two children. Weekly grocery budget: $80. Payment context: SNAP-like food benefits, so avoid hot prepared foods, restaurant meals, delivery fees, and non-food items. Health constraint: the adult has hypertension, so reduce sodium where possible. Store access: no car, shops at one nearby supermarket. Cooking constraint: limited time and basic kitchen. Output: list items, quantities, total cost, health rationale, and any tradeoffs. Do not recommend products outside the product list. 7. It should first measure observable properties of AI outputs. The main outcome groups are stability, constraint compliance, healthfulness, affordability, feasibility, transparency, and equity. 8.1 Stability Stability measures whether the same persona receives similar recommendations across repeated AI interactions. This is the most distinctive part of the paper because it directly studies AI dynamicity. Item-level Jaccard similarity: the overlap between two recommended baskets for the same persona, model, and prompt. Category-level similarity: overlap in food categories when exact products differ. Nutrition coefficient of variation: within-cell variation in sodium, sugar, saturated fat, fiber, protein, and total calories. Cost coefficient of variation: within-cell variation in total basket cost. Recommendation entropy: diversity of product categories or key recommended products across repeated runs. A high-quality AI food assistant does not need to recommend identical baskets every time, but it should generate stable outcomes under the same constraints. Large variation in cost, sodium, or SNAP compliance would be policy-relevant even if item-level variation is acceptable. 8.2 Constraint Compliance Constraint compliance is measured by whether the basket violates explicit persona constraints. Constraint Possible metric Budget Budget violation indicator; budget use ratio = total cost / budget. SNAP-like eligibility Share of basket cost spent on ineligible or questionable items. Hypertension Sodium per 1,000 kcal; sodium threshold violation. Diabetes Added sugar or refined carbohydrate proxy; sugar threshold violation. Allergy or dietary restriction Allergen violation indicator, if included in persona. Cooking constraint Preparation burden score; share of items requiring complex cooking. Access constraint Number of implied stores; reliance on specialty products. 8.3 Healthfulness Healthfulness should be reported using transparent basket-level nutrition metrics rather than relying only on a single composite score. Candidate metrics include calories, sodium per 1,000 kcal, sugar per 1,000 kcal, saturated fat, fiber, protein, fruit and vegetable share, and ultra-processed food share. A supplementary HEI-like score can be constructed, but it should not replace the component-level outcomes. 8.4 Affordability and Feasibility Affordability is central to the food policy contribution. A healthy basket that exceeds the budget is not a feasible recommendation for low-income households. Total basket cost. Budget use ratio. Probability of exceeding budget. Cost per calorie. Cost per gram of protein. Cost per fruit and vegetable serving. Price premium relative to a baseline low-cost basket. Feasibility can be coded using a simple score based on availability, preparation burden, number of stores required, ingredient familiarity, storage burden, and suitability for the household. This score can be partially rule-based and partially hand-coded, with inter-coder checks if the project expands. 8.5 Transparency and Explanation Quality AI systems do not only recommend items; they also explain them. Explanation quality can be coded with a transparency score. For each output, record whether the model explains health rationale, price rationale, SNAP-like feasibility, preparation feasibility, substitution logic, uncertainty, and health-price tradeoffs. 8.6 Equity Equity is measured by comparing outcomes across personas. The main question is whether constrained households receive systematically worse recommendations. For example, do low-income or SNAP personas receive baskets that are less healthy, more likely to exceed budget, less feasible, or more unstable than middle-income personas? The equity analysis should avoid claiming that personas represent real demographic groups. Instead, they represent policy-relevant constraint bundles. The equity concern is about unequal quality of algorithmic assistance under unequal household constraints. 9. Empirical Framework The empirical analysis can remain simple and transparent. The main model can be written as: Y_{p,m,k,r} = alpha + Persona_p + Model_m + Prompt_k + epsilon_{p,m,k,r} where Y is an outcome such as basket cost, budget violation, sodium, stability, or transparency; p indexes persona; m indexes model; k indexes prompt type; and r indexes repeated run. The paper can also estimate models with interactions: Y_{p,m,k,r} = alpha + LowIncome_p + SNAP_p + HealthConstraint_p + AccessConstraint_p + Model_m + Prompt_k + epsilon_{p,m,k,r} For binary outcomes such as budget violation or SNAP violation, linear probability models are acceptable for transparency, with logit/probit as robustness checks. For continuous outcomes, use OLS with standard errors clustered by persona-model-prompt cell or bootstrapped across repeated runs. A particularly useful analysis is variance decomposition. It can show whether variation in outcomes is mainly driven by persona differences, model differences, prompt differences, or within-cell dynamic variation. If within-cell variation is large, that directly supports the claim that dynamicity itself is policy-relevant. The pilot should use a product database of around 50 to 80 items. The goal is not publication-ready precision. The goal is to see whether the audit produces meaningful variation and whether coding is manageable. Pilot step Task Output 1 Build a small grocery database with price, category, nutrition, SNAP-like eligibility, and preparation burden. The paper should not be presented as a technology novelty paper. It should be presented as a food information policy and algorithmic governance paper. Food Policy prioritizes empirical evidence that informs food policy debates, especially economic and social aspects of food-sector policy. This project fits if it shows that AI food assistants may become part of the consumer food choice environment and that their outputs need policy-relevant evaluation before deployment in grocery platforms or public nutrition settings. The policy relevance is especially strong if the paper emphasizes low-income and SNAP-relevant constraints. AI may be promoted as a tool to help households eat healthier on a budget. But if AI-generated advice frequently exceeds budgets, ignores eligibility constraints, or recommends unrealistic baskets, then policymakers and platforms should not treat AI guidance as a neutral extension of nutrition labeling. The strongest Food Policy framing is: AI food assistants are a new food information infrastructure. The paper provides early empirical evidence on whether this infrastructure is stable, affordable, and equitable for constrained households. 14. Draft Abstract This paper studies dynamic information customization in food choice by auditing AI-generated grocery recommendations. Unlike static nutrition labels, AI food assistants generate adaptive and personalized advice that may vary across consumers, prompts, models, and repeated interactions. We construct a set of policy-relevant household personas varying by income, SNAP-like payment constraints, health conditions, food access, language, and cooking capacity. For each persona, we ask multiple AI models to generate weekly grocery baskets using a fixed product database and repeated prompt conditions. We evaluate the resulting baskets along five dimensions: stability, constraint compliance, healthfulness, affordability, and equity. The audit provides evidence on whether AI-generated grocery advice can serve as a reliable food information tool for constrained households. The findings speak to food information policy, online grocery governance, and the use of AI in nutrition assistance contexts. 16. Draft Introduction Opening Food information policy has traditionally focused on disclosure. Nutrition facts panels, front-of-package labels, and health claims are designed to make product attributes visible to consumers. The rise of generative AI changes this policy environment. AI food assistants do not merely disclose information. They interpret product attributes, rank alternatives, explain substitutions, and can generate entire grocery baskets tailored to a household’s budget, health conditions, language, and cooking constraints. This shift creates a new policy question: when food information is dynamically customized by algorithms, is it stable, feasible, and equitable? This question matters because the households that may benefit most from customized food guidance often face the strongest constraints. Low-income and SNAP-relevant households must manage tight budgets, store access, transportation limits, cooking constraints, and chronic disease risks. A grocery recommendation that is nutritionally desirable but too expensive, not eligible under food assistance rules, or unrealistic to prepare is not a meaningful improvement in food choice. Therefore, AI-generated food advice should be evaluated not only by whether it sounds healthy, but by whether it produces affordable and feasible baskets under real-world constraints. 中文研究 Demo 1. 核心摘要 暂定题目： 动态信息定制与食品选择：AI 生成购物篮推荐的算法审计。 这个项目研究的是一种新的食品信息形态：AI 生成的 grocery advice。传统营养标签是静态披露，所有人看到的信息基本一样；AI 推荐则不同，它会根据用户画像、预算、健康约束、语言、烹饪条件和 prompt 写法动态生成建议。同一个 persona、同一个问题，重复问多次也可能得到不同购物篮。这个动态性本身就是一个值得研究的问题。 这个项目第一阶段不需要做真人实验。主要数据就是 AI 生成的 grocery basket。每一个 observation 可以定义为 persona × model × prompt × run。也就是说，我们固定消费者画像、模型、prompt 类型，然后重复运行，直接审计 AI 输出是否稳定、是否符合预算、是否考虑 SNAP-like eligibility、是否健康、是否现实可执行，以及是否对低收入或约束更多的家庭产生系统性劣势。 这个 idea 的关键不是“AI 会不会给健康建议”，而是“AI 是否能在真实食品政策约束下给出稳定、可负担、公平、可执行的建议”。这样 framing 才更像 Food Policy，而不是普通 AI nutrition paper。 一句话 idea AI 正在从“营养信息披露工具”变成“食品选择中介”。本文审计 AI 生成的食品购物建议在预算、SNAP、健康和 food access 约束下是否稳定、可负担、合规且公平。 2. 和已有文献的关系 Balcombe et al. reviewer 会觉得结果不可复现。可以先构建 100 到 200 个常见 grocery products，包括谷物、乳制品、蛋白质、蔬菜水果、饮料、零食、冷冻食品、罐头和预制食品。每个商品记录 category、price、package size、basic nutrition、SNAP-like eligibility 和 preparation burden。 主设计可以是 12 personas × 3 models × 3 prompt types × 20 runs，一共 2,160 个 AI-generated baskets。pilot 可以先做 6 personas × 2 models × 2 prompt types × 10 runs，一共 240 个 baskets。 设计部分 建议 原因 Persona 主分析 12 个；pilot 6 个 足够覆盖低收入、SNAP、健康、交通、烹饪、语言约束，但不至于失控。 Model 2 到 3 个 LLM 避免文章变成单一模型测试。 Prompt 3 类 prompt 测试约束写得越明确，输出是否更稳定、更合规。 重复次数 每个 cell 20 次 可以衡量同一条件下的动态波动。 商品库 100 到 200 个商品 兼顾可控性、真实感和 token 成本。 7. Persona 设计 Persona 不要写得太文学化，不要加入太多不可控背景故事。应该采用结构化信息，例如 household size、weekly budget、SNAP status、health constraint、store access、cooking capacity 和 language。主分析 12 个 persona 比较合适。 编号 Persona 研究目的 1 中等收入成年人，无健康约束，有车，完整厨房 基准组。 2 低收入成年人，无 SNAP，无健康约束 低收入但没有 SNAP 的情况。 3 低收入 SNAP 成年人，无健康约束 测试 SNAP-like 支付约束。 4 低收入 SNAP 家庭，两个孩子 测试家庭预算压力和儿童友好食品。 5 低收入 SNAP，无车，城市环境 测试交通约束和单店采购可行性。 6 低收入 SNAP，农村，门店可及性弱 测试 rural food access。 7 低收入 SNAP，高血压 测试低收入下的低钠约束。 8 低收入 SNAP，糖尿病 测试低收入下的糖和精制碳水约束。 9 低收入 SNAP，只能微波炉或烹饪能力有限 测试 preparation feasibility。 10 低收入老年人，高血压，行动不便 测试年龄、慢性病和 mobility 约束。 11 西语低收入 SNAP 家庭 测试语言可及性和文化可执行性。 12 中等收入、健康意识强、糖尿病消费者 区分健康约束和贫困约束。 pilot 可以先用 6 个：中等收入基准；低收入 SNAP 成年人；低收入 SNAP 有孩子家庭；低收入 SNAP 高血压；低收入 SNAP 糖尿病；低收入 SNAP 无车或农村 access constraint。 8. 衡量指标 第一步不要直接衡量消费者福利，因为那会变得很难。应该先把 AI 输出本身测清楚。主要 outcome 可以分成七类。 8.1 动态性和稳定性 这是文章最有新意的部分。同一个 persona、同一个 model、同一个 prompt，重复运行 20 次，看结果是否稳定。 Item-level Jaccard similarity：两个购物篮的商品重合度。 Category-level similarity：即使商品不同，食品类别是否相似。 Nutrition CV：同一 cell 下 sodium、sugar、fiber、protein、calories 的变异系数。 Cost CV：同一 cell 下总价格的变异系数。 Recommendation entropy：推荐商品或类别的分散程度。 注意，AI 不需要每次推荐完全一样的商品。但是如果同一个约束下，有时推荐 $70，有时推荐 $115，或者 sodium 波动非常大，这就是政策问题。 8.2 约束合规 约束合规是最硬的指标。给定约束以后，检查 AI 有没有违反。 约束 衡量方式 预算 是否超过预算；total cost / budget。 SNAP-like eligibility 是否推荐热食、餐厅食品、配送费、非食品等不应包含的项目。 高血压 sodium per 1,000 kcal；是否超过预设阈值。 糖尿病 sugar 或 refined carbs proxy；是否超过预设阈值。 过敏或饮食限制 是否推荐违反限制的商品。 烹饪能力 是否推荐大量复杂烹饪食品。 食物可及性 是否需要多个商店或特殊商品。 8.3 健康性 健康性最好不要只用一个综合分数。应该报告 component-level nutrition metrics，包括 calories、sodium per 1,000 kcal、sugar per 1,000 kcal、saturated fat、fiber、protein、fruit/vegetable share 和 ultra-processed food share。可以额外构造一个 HEI-like score，但不要让它成为唯一结果。 8.4 可负担性和现实可执行性 这是 Food Policy 贡献最重要的一部分。一个健康但超预算的购物篮，对低收入家庭来说没有意义。 Total basket cost。 Budget use ratio。 Budget violation rate。 Cost per calorie。 Cost per gram protein。 Cost per fruit/vegetable serving。 相对低成本 baseline basket 的价格溢价。 可执行性可以用 feasibility score 衡量，考虑 availability、preparation burden、number of stores、ingredient familiarity、storage burden 和 household suitability。 8.5 透明度和解释质量 AI 推荐不仅有商品列表，还有解释。可以记录模型是否解释了健康原因、价格原因、SNAP-like 可行性、烹饪可行性、替代逻辑、不确定性和 health-price tradeoff。然后构造 Transparency Score。 8.6 公平性 公平性不是简单比较 race 或 gender。这里更适合比较不同 constraint bundle 的输出质量。比如低收入、SNAP、无车、rural、limited cooking 或有慢性病的 persona 是否得到更贵、更不健康、更不稳定或更不可执行的推荐。 这个点很重要，因为 AI 可能表面上是个性化，但个性化不一定等于公平。它可能对信息完整、预算充足、限制较少的人表现很好，但对真正需要帮助的人表现不稳定。 9. 实证框架 主模型可以保持简单，重点是透明和可解释。 Y_{p,m,k,r} = alpha + Persona_p + Model_m + Prompt_k + epsilon_{p,m,k,r} 其中 Y 可以是 basket cost、budget violation、sodium、stability、feasibility 或 transparency。p 是 persona，m 是 model，k 是 prompt type，r 是 repeated run。 还可以估计约束变量模型： Y_{p,m,k,r} = alpha + LowIncome_p + SNAP_p + HealthConstraint_p + AccessConstraint_p + Model_m + Prompt_k + epsilon_{p,m,k,r} 二元 outcome，比如 budget violation 或 SNAP violation，可以先用 linear probability model，robustness 再用 logit/probit。连续 outcome 用 OLS。 一个很有价值的分析是 variance decomposition。它可以回答：输出差异主要来自 persona、model、prompt，还是同一个 cell 里的 repeated run randomness？如果 repeated run 的方差很大，就说明 AI 动态性本身是 food policy 问题。 10. 最小可行 pilot 最快可以先做 240 个 AI 输出：6 personas、2 models、2 prompt types、10 runs。商品库先用 50 到 80 个商品。pilot 的目标不是直接投稿，而是验证这个设计能不能产生有意义的 variation，编码是否可控，结果是否有政策含义。 步骤 任务 产出 1 建立小型商品库，包含价格、类别、营养、SNAP-like eligibility 和 preparation burden。 50 到 80 个商品的 家庭是否稳定、可负担、公平、可执行”。 Food Policy 关心的是食品部门政策的制定、实施和评估，以及食品系统的经济和社会层面。如果本文把 AI 放到 food information policy、online grocery governance、nutrition assistance 和 low-income food choice 里面，就有比较强的政策相关性。 最强的政策含义是：AI 不能被简单看作更高级的 nutrition label。因为 label 是静态披露，而 AI 是动态中介。它可能降低信息成本，也可能生成不可执行的建议，甚至对约束更多的家庭表现更差。因此，未来如果平台或公共营养项目想用 AI 做 food guidance，就需要审计标准。 14. 建议时间线 阶段 大概时间 产出 Pilot design 3 到 5 天 assistant 会根据消费者画像、prompt、模型和重复交互动态生成个性化建议。本文构建一组与食品政策相关的 household personas，覆盖收入、SNAP-like 支付约束、健康条件、食物可及性、语言和烹饪能力差异。我们要求多个 AI 模型在固定商品库中为每个 persona 生成一周购物篮，并在重复运行中评估推荐结果。主要 outcome 包括稳定性、约束合规、健康性、可负担性和公平性。本文为 AI 食品推荐是否可以作为可靠的食品信息工具提供早期经验证据，并对食品信息政策、在线 grocery 平台治理和营养援助项目中的 AI 使用具有政策意义。 16. 中文 introduction 开头草稿 传统食品信息政策主要关注信息披露。营养成分表、front-of-package label 和健康声称的核心功能，是让消费者看到产品的营养属性。生成式 AI 的出现改变了这个政策环境。AI food assistant 不只是披露信息，而是解释产品属性、排序替代品、说明替换理由，并可以根据家庭预算、健康状况、语言和烹饪条件生成完整购物篮。这个转变带来一个新的政策问题：当食品信息由算法动态定制时，这些建议是否稳定、可负担、可执行且公平？ 这个问题尤其关系到低收入和 SNAP-relevant 家庭。理论上，这些家庭最可能从个性化食品建议中受益，因为他们面临预算、交通、门店可及性、烹饪时间和慢性病风险等多重约束。但如果 AI 生成的建议虽然看起来健康，却超出预算、不符合 SNAP-like eligibility，或者需要复杂烹饪和多个商店采购，那么这种建议并不能真正改善食品选择。因此，评价 AI 食品建议不能只看它是否听起来健康，而要看它是否在真实约束下稳定、现实和公平。 References and Source Notes Balcombe, K., Fraser, I., Lowe, B., &amp; Souza Monteiro, D.
